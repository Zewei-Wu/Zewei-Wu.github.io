My object is the **Large Magellanic Cloud** (LMC), an irregular satellite galaxy
living in the neighbourhood of our own Milky Way — which is exactly what makes
it such a useful target for astronomers.

Besides its proximity, the LMC is notable for being classified as a *Magellanic
spiral*: it has only one spiral arm. That suggests it was once a dwarf spiral
whose arms were disrupted, most likely through tidal interaction with the Small
Magellanic Cloud (SMC) and the Milky Way.

## Properties of the LMC

| Property | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Distance | $\approx 50$ kpc (163,000 ly) — one of the closest satellites of the Milky Way |
| Type | Dwarf galaxy, classified as a Magellanic spiral |
| Number of stars | $\sim 2 \times 10^{10}$ |
| Baryonic mass | $\sim 1 \times 10^{10}\,M_\odot$ |
| Total mass | $\sim 1.38 \times 10^{11}\,M_\odot$, including dark matter |
| Diameter | $\approx 9.86$ kpc ($\approx 32{,}200$ ly) |
| X-ray luminosity (1.5–12 keV) | $\sim 6 \times 10^{31}$ W |

There is good evidence for a history of tidal interaction between the two
Magellanic Clouds — most obviously the bridge of gas connecting them. Captured
into orbit around the Milky Way, both are also believed to have been disrupted
by our own galaxy, which likely contributes to their irregular shapes.

## Dwarf galaxies in general

The LMC belongs to the broader category of irregular dwarf galaxies. These are
characterised by their chaotic appearance, often the result of gravitational
interaction with other galaxies. They typically contain a few billion stars and
have no regular shape.

| Property | Typical dwarf galaxy |
| --- | --- |
| Number of stars | A few $\times 10^{9}$, against $\sim 10^{11}$ for the Milky Way |
| Mass | $\sim 10^{9}\,M_\odot$ total |
| Size | Usually under 3 kpc (the Milky Way is $\sim 30$ kpc) |
| Bolometric luminosity | $10^{5}$ to $10^{7}\,L_\odot$ |

One important caveat: dwarf galaxies are frequently found to be baryon-deficient,
because ionising radiation strips them of gas. They therefore have much higher
mass-to-light ratios than larger galaxies — the majority of their mass is dark
matter rather than stars. The exact ratio varies considerably with local
conditions and with the measurement method used. Mass and size in the dwarf
regime are also heavily convention-dependent, so quoted numbers should be
compared carefully.

Judged against these numbers, the LMC is on the large side for a dwarf galaxy in
mass, size and luminosity alike.

## How common are they?

Irregular dwarf galaxies are common in the universe, and are often found in
groups or clusters where interactions drive their irregular shapes. The exact
number density is hard to pin down, partly because it varies strongly with
environment, and partly because low-mass dwarfs are genuinely difficult to
distinguish from other objects such as globular clusters.

Recent surveys using signatures like Lyman-alpha emission are improving the
picture, but it has been estimated that around 90% of dwarf galaxies remain
undetected. That number should fall as observational facilities improve.
